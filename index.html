How this is calculated

For an object at distance u, a lens of focal length f focused at distance s with aperture N (f-number) produces a blur spot (circle of confusion) on the sensor of diameter: 

c(u) = f² · |u − s| / (N · u · (s − f))

This is a thin-lens result and depends only on the lens and focus settings — sensor size doesn't change the physical blur spot. Sensor size does determine what counts as "acceptably sharp": each row's acceptable circle of confusion is the conventional sensor diagonal / 1500, shown in the table. The two distances where a row's curve crosses that value are its near and far depth-of-field limits, drawn as the pair of dashed vertical lines in the row's color; everything between them reads as in focus, and that span is the depth of field. Equivalently, with hyperfocal distance H = f²/(N·c) + f, they are s(H−f)/(H+s−2f) and s(H−f)/(H−s) — the far limit running to infinity once you focus at or beyond H.

Sensor size also sets the horizontal field of view for a given focal length: FOV = 2·atan(sensor width / (2f)). A smaller sensor "crops" the same lens to a narrower FOV, which is why a 50mm lens looks more telephoto on Micro Four Thirds than on full frame.

The "35mm equivalent" column applies that same crop factor (full-frame diagonal / sensor diagonal) to both focal length and aperture, giving the full-frame settings that would frame the same shot with a comparable depth of field — the usual way of comparing gear across formats.

By default the graph plots raw blur diameter on the sensor itself, which depends only on the actual (non-equivalent) focal length, aperture, and distance — a smaller sensor's lens can produce a physically smaller blur spot even when its equivalent aperture is "faster." The Normalize blur to full-frame equivalent option (in the graph's display settings) multiplies every curve and threshold by its row's crop factor, matching what you'd actually see comparing final images enlarged to the same output size — this is what makes the graph agree with the 35mm-equivalent column.

The formula above is geometric (ray) optics and lets the curve reach a true zero at the focus distance. In reality, diffraction through the aperture spreads even a perfectly focused point into a small disk — a wave-optics floor that geometric optics doesn't model, roughly 2.44 · λ · N in diameter (λ ≈ 550nm, standing in for white light), independent of focal length, sensor size, or distance. The Include diffraction blur option combines this floor with the geometric curve in quadrature, sqrt(defocus² + diffraction²) — the usual approximation for combining two independent blur sources. Past a sensor's diffraction-limited aperture (where the diffraction floor alone already exceeds its acceptable CoC), no distance registers as "in focus" by that criterion, so the near/far markers and focus-range lane disappear for rows stopped down that far.

The Focus distance control (top of the table) can be switched to Subject %, which sets every row's distance indirectly, by how much of the frame a subject should fill, instead of a typed distance. At distance d, the frame's physical width is 2 · d · tan(HFOV/2) (rectilinear projection); setting a subject of width W to fill a given percentage p of that frame width and solving for d gives: 

d = W / (2 · (p/100) · tan(HFOV/2))

using each row's own horizontal FOV. Subject size (W) is a single value next to the mode control, shared by every row — the same physical subject, framed differently per camera. Each row keeps its own percentage, and its computed distance updates automatically alongside it whenever subject size, percentage, sensor, or focal length changes; the distance itself is read-only, derived rather than typed. In this mode the table's "Focus distance" column relabels to "Relative to Frame Subject Size," and "lock focus distance across rows" ties the percentages together instead of the distances — so every row frames the subject identically, each from whatever distance its own field of view requires.
